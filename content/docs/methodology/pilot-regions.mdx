A region's index is only as good as the rainfall data behind it. Before Mvua trusts a region, its calibration has to hold up on a second, independent dataset. This page lists every region checked so far, 36 sites in 12 countries, and the result.

## The test

The [index methodology](/docs/methodology/index-methodology) calibrates each region on ERA5, a weather reanalysis. To check it, each season is recomputed from **CHIRPS**, a satellite and rain gauge blend at about 5 km, over the same years and the same season window. Two questions decide the result:

1. **Do the two datasets rise and fall together?** The correlation of the season totals, where 1.0 means they move in lockstep.
2. **Do they agree on the droughts?** How many of the 6 driest seasons in ERA5 are also among the 6 driest in CHIRPS. These are the seasons the index pays in, so this matters most.

A region is **confirmed** when the season totals correlate at 0.7 or more and the two datasets share at least 4 of their 6 driest seasons. It is **borderline** at 0.6 or more with 3 shared: worth a closer look, for example with a different season window, but not yet trusted. Only confirmed regions can have their index frozen and be offered in a pilot.

## What the checks found

**Kano.** The two datasets disagree on which seasons were dry. ERA5's driest Kano season, 2021, which the calibrated index pays in full, is an ordinary season in CHIRPS (16th driest of 34). CHIRPS's driest, 2007, is only 10th driest in ERA5. Satellite greenness agrees with neither, plausibly because of irrigation. With no agreement and no referee, the chance of paying the wrong seasons is too high for a first pilot. Kano still hosts the daily testnet publisher run, which tests operations, not calibration.

**Southern Nigeria.** Lagos, Anambra and Delta are wet enough that a rainfall shortfall rarely hurts crops, and the datasets disagree there too. These regions are better candidates for a flood or excess rain index later.

**West Africa.** None of the 16 West African sites pass: 14 across Nigeria's north, middle belt and south, plus northern Ghana and Senegal. The rural Kano site agrees even less than Kano city, so the city result is not an urban effect. West African rain falls mostly as short, intense local storms, which a global reanalysis like ERA5 smooths away. The problem is the dataset meeting this kind of rain, not any one state. A West African pilot needs an index calibrated on CHIRPS or rain gauges, with publishers reporting from the same source.

**Where ERA5 works.** It holds up where rain comes from large weather systems over flatter ground: Southern Africa's single long season (Zambia, Zimbabwe), Kenya's south east and central Tanzania. It fails near mountains and Lake Victoria (Eldoret, Meru, Mbale, Arusha), where rain is too local for a 25 km model.

**Dry spells.** Both datasets were also compared on the longest run of dry days in each season. Agreement is weak almost everywhere, because ERA5 tends to record light drizzle on dry days. A dry days index therefore needs CHIRPS or rain gauges, not ERA5.

**Why calibration stays on ERA5.** ERA5 and CHIRPS differ in level by region (ERA5 often reads 20 to 40% lower), so the two cannot be mixed. The live publishers report the same ECMWF model family that ERA5 comes from, corroborated by GFS, so calibrating on ERA5 keeps the history and the live data consistent. CHIRPS serves as the independent check.

## Next candidates

1. **Choma and Masvingo** are calibrated and priced; their indices can be frozen on testnet like Nakuru and Dodoma.
2. **Machakos and Kitui** are rechecked with other season windows.
3. **West Africa, including Nigeria,** returns once a CHIRPS based index and publisher source exist. The first step is checking CHIRPS against satellite greenness in Nigeria.

## Reproduce

CHIRPS seasons are fetched from the public ClimateSERV API and compared with the ERA5 files in [`mvua-contract/data/history`](https://github.com/Mvua-Protocol/mvua-contract/tree/main/data/history), season by season.
